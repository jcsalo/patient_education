## Jejunostomy

The topic for this video is a feeding jejunostomy

## 

We will start with a review of anatomy. Food travels from the esophagus to the stomach, and finally into the jejunum, the first part of the small intestine.

## 

Cancers in the esophagus or stomach tend to grow for a long period of time without symptoms.

With time, however, they can cause difficulty with eating and in extreme cases can cause weight loss

Some patients with cancer of the esophagus or stomach can benefit from a feeding tube to help with nutrition

## 

There are two basic kinds of feeding tubes:

A gastrostomy tube is placed through the skin into the stomach

A jejunostomy tube is placed through the skin into the small intestine

## 

This video focuses on the feeding jejunostomy

## Stomach

The stomach acts as a temporary storage area for food, like this grain elevator

## Jejunum

= Small Intestine

The stomach sends a small amount of food at a time into the jejunum, much like this grain elevator sends food onto this conveyor belt.

## 

Just like a conveyor belt, it’s easy for the jejunum to get overloaded with food.

## 

… and if it’s overloaded, it will start to complain.

## 

So when you have a feeding jejunostomy, it’s important that the formula goes into the tube as a slow, steady rate in order to avoid overwhelming the jejunum. This require a pump to administer the feeding over a long period of time.

## How Much Formula Do I Need?

An important question is “How much formula do I need?”

## 

The short answer is that your dietitian will calculate how much formula you need, but we can give you a rough idea.

## Typical Tube Feedings 150lbs+

People with a body weight over 150 pounds usually need about 5 cartons of feeds.

Each carton is about 240mL

So the total amount is 1250 mL

## Typical Tube Feedings 150lbs+

This is usually administered over about 16 hours, which requires a rate of 75mL/hour

## Typical Tube Feedings 150lbs+

In the hospital we run the tube feeds from 6pm to 10am, but a home you can of course use whatever schedule works best for you.

## Typical Tube Feedings \<150lbs

Those with a body weight less than 150 pounds generally need 4 cartons of feeds.

Each carton is 240mL, so the total is 960mL

## Typical Tube Feedings \<150lbs

This can be administered over 16 hours at a rate of 60mL/hour

## Typical Tube Feedings \<150lbs

Again this usually is from 6pm to 10am

## Jejunostomy Tube

The jejunostomy tube can also be used to administer water.

## Jejunostomy Tube

## 

Some pumps come with two bags, one for formula and one for water.

Your care team will tell you how much water to use, but it’s generally around one quart or 1000mL per day total

## 

It’s also possible to use a syringe to administer water

## 

Water can be flushed into the jejunostomy tube slowly with a syringe.

While it won’t work to administer a syringe full of formula into a jejunostomy tube, it is usually fine to inject a syringe full of water.

## Medicines which can’t be crushed

A word of caution, however, as some pills can’t be crushed:

Extended release, Sustained release, or Controlled release

## Medicines which can’t be crushed

In addition, Flomax, or tamsulosin, can’t be given via a tube.

The same with liquid-filled capsules and soft-gel capsules.

Your pharmacist can help here
