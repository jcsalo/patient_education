## Jejunostomy

The topic for this video is a feeding jejunostomy

## Slide 3

We will start with a review of anatomy.  Food travels from the esophagus to the stomach, and finally into the jejunum, the first part of the small intestine.

## Slide 4

Cancers in the esophagus or stomach tend to grow for a long period of time without symptoms.

With time, however, they can cause difficulty with eating and in extreme cases can cause weight loss

Some patients with cancer of the esophagus or stomach can benefit from a feeding tube to help with nutrition

## Slide 5

There are two basic kinds of feeding tubes:

A gastrostomy tube is placed through the skin into the stomach

A jejunostomy tube is placed through the skin into the small intestine

This video focuses on the feeding  jejunostomy

## Jejunum
= Small Intestine

The stomach sends a small amount of food at a time into the jejunum, much like this grain elevator sends grain onto this conveyor belt.

## Slide 13

Just like a conveyor belt, it’s easy for the jejunum to get overloaded with food.

## Slide 14

… and if it’s overloaded, it will start to complain.

## Slide 15

So when you have a feeding jejunostomy, it’s important that the formula goes into the tube as a slow, steady rate in order to avoid overloading the jejunum. 
This requires a pump to administer the feeding over a several hours.

## How Much Formula Do I Need?

An important question is “How much formula do I need?”

## Slide 19

The short answer is that your dietitian will calculate how much formula you need, but we can give you a rough idea.

## Rate and Duration

Your dietitian will calculate how many cartons of tube feeds you will need

For instance, it that’s 5 cartons and each carton is 240mL, the total will be 1200mL per day

One method would be to run the tube feeds continuously, 24 hours per day.

This would require a rate of 50mL per hour, because 50 x 14 is 1200.

## 5 cartons Tube feeds 1200mL

So we could run tube feeds 24 hours per day at 50mL/hour.  This would work fine…

## 5 cartons Tube feeds

The problem with this is it’s inconvenient. 

If you want to go for a walk or go shopping, you would need to take your pump with you,

## Slide 23

If you can avoid it, you don’t want to feel like you’re chained to your infusion pump.

## 5 cartons Tube feeds

An alternative would be to increase the rate and decrease the time.
You could increase the rate to 75mL/hour, which would only take 16 hours.  You would have 8 hours a day disconnected from the pump.

## 5 cartons Tube feeds

So what if you wanted to get the feeding in within 12 hours?  You would need to run the pump at 100mL/hour.

For many patients, this won’t work, because the small intestine can’t handle that much formula at a time


## Slide 26

Too high a rate of tube feeds will overwhelm the jejunum, and the result is cramps and diarrhea. 

## 5 cartons Tube feeds

A slow rate of 50mL/hour is inconvenient, as it may require being hooked up to the pump all day.

On the other hand, a faster rate of 100mL/hr  would be more convenient, but carries the risk of diarrhea and cramps.

## 5 cartons Tube feeds

Running tube feeds for 16 hours a day is for many patients a good compromise between convenience and good tolerance of tube feeds. Most patients can tolerate 75mL/hour of tube feeds.

## Typical Tube Feedings 150lbs+

People with a body weight over 150 pounds usually need about 5 cartons of feeds.

Each carton is about 240mL

So the total amount is 1200 mL

## Typical Tube Feedings 150lbs+

This is usually administered at a rate of 75mL/hour for 16 hours, which totals 1200mL



## Typical Tube Feedings 150lbs+

In the hospital we run the tube feeds from 6pm to 10am, but a home you can of course use whatever schedule works best for you.

## Typical Tube Feedings <150lbs

Those with a body weight less than 150 pounds generally need 4 cartons of feeds.

Each carton is 240mL, so the total is 960mL

## Typical Tube Feedings <150lbs

This can be administered at a rate of 60mL/hour over 16 hours

## Typical Tube Feedings <150lbs

Again this usually is from 6pm to 10am

## Starting Tube Feeds

When starting tube feeds for the first time it is important to start slowly to allow the small intestine, the jejunum, to get adjusted.

We typically start at 30mL/hour and run them continuously, meaning 24 hours per day. 

## Starting Tube Feeds

We would then increase 10mL/hour every 8-12 hours

## Typical Tube Feed Plans

So a typical plan tube feeds is 16 hours per day, which in our example is 6pm to 10am

For patients who need 5 cartons of feeds, getting the feeding done in 16 hours requires a rate of 75mL/hour.

For those needing 4 cartons, getting the feeding done in 16 hours requires a rate of 60mL/hour

## Jejunostomy Tube

The jejunostomy tube can also be used to administer water.

## Water

In addition to formula, water is required to prevent dehydration.

Most patients need about a liter of water a day, which can be taken either by mouth of through the feeding tube

One way to do this is taking 240mL of water 4 times per day.

## Slide 50

Some pumps come with two bags, one for formula and one for water.

Your care team will tell you how much water to use, but it’s generally around one quart or 1000mL per day total


## Slide 51

It’s also possible to use a syringe to administer water

## Slide 52

Water can be flushed into the jejunostomy tube slowly with a syringe.


While it won’t work to administer a syringe full of *formula* into a jejunostomy tube, it is usually fine to inject a syringe full of water.
